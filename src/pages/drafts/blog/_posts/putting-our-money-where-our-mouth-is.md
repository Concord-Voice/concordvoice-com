We write a lot about privacy. We've published posts about government surveillance, corporate data harvesting, the erosion of encryption rights, and the legal battles being waged over your right to a private conversation. We've explained how our architecture is designed to protect you. We've been pretty vocal about where we stand.

But talk is cheap, and principles that don't cost you anything aren't really principles. So let's talk about what we're actually doing about it.

## The Electronic Frontier Foundation

If you spend any time in the privacy and digital rights space, you know who the EFF is. If you don't, the short version: they're the oldest and most effective digital civil liberties organization in the world, founded in 1990 when most lawmakers didn't know what the internet was. They've been fighting the legal and policy battles that protect your rights online ever since.

They were there when the government tried to mandate the Clipper Chip, a backdoor built into encryption hardware in the 1990s. They fought it. They won. They've fought SOPA, PIPA, FISA Section 702, EARN IT, KOSA, the UK's Online Safety Act, the EU's Chat Control proposals, and more government and corporate overreaches than we have space to list. They run Surveillance Self-Defense. They built Let's Encrypt (in partnership with others), which gave the entire internet free TLS certificates and helped push HTTPS adoption from a luxury to a standard. They file amicus briefs, litigate, publish, educate, and don't take money from the companies they hold accountable.

They also threatened to sue the NSA over PRISM, which, honestly, is just cool.

## The Citizen Lab

If the EFF is the legal and policy arm of the digital rights movement, the Citizen Lab is the intelligence unit. Based at the University of Toronto's Munk School of Global Affairs, they do the kind of technical research that exposes what governments and surveillance vendors are actually doing to people, not in theory, not as a policy argument, but with forensic evidence.

They're the team that exposed NSO Group's Pegasus spyware, which was used to target journalists, activists, lawyers, and heads of state across 45 countries. They uncovered FinFisher. They documented how China's Great Firewall works at a technical level. They've published reports on how authoritarian governments use commercial surveillance tools to track dissidents abroad, how VPN apps sold as "privacy" tools were exfiltrating user data to Chinese servers, and how SMS-based voter suppression worked in specific elections.

The reason this matters for us specifically: Citizen Lab research is often what proves that the "we only use this for catching criminals" argument for surveillance tools is false. Every time a government says, "trust us, we won't abuse this capability," and then abuses it, the Citizen Lab is usually the organization that finds the forensic evidence. That kind of independent technical accountability is what keeps the conversation about encryption and surveillance grounded in facts rather than assurances.

They also run a fellowship program that trains the next generation of researchers doing this work, and they operate the Citizen Lab Fund specifically to support independent digital security research. That's what we're donating to.

## Stripe Climate

Running a communications platform takes infrastructure, and infrastructure has a carbon footprint. Servers, cooling, bandwidth, power; it adds up, and pretending it doesn't is the kind of greenwashing we'd rather not participate in.

Through Stripe Climate, a portion of revenue is directed to carbon removal after our monthly operational costs are covered. Not offsets, removal. There's a meaningful difference. Carbon offsets are often a way to pay someone else not to emit carbon somewhere else, which is fine in theory but notoriously difficult to verify in practice. Carbon removal is what it sounds like: pulling CO₂ that's already in the atmosphere back out. Stripe Climate pools contributions from businesses like us to fund a portfolio of removal approaches, from direct air capture and enhanced rock weathering to ocean-based methods, and others, that are currently expensive and need early buyers to develop at scale.

The "after operational costs are covered" part is deliberate. We're not going to make promises we can't keep. If we haven't covered the lights-on costs for the month, this doesn't kick in. When we have, it does. We're a small team building something we believe in, and we'd rather be honest about the condition than quietly pause contributions and never mention it.

## Why We're Doing All of This

We didn't build Concord Voice in a vacuum. Every architectural decision we've made, like E2EE by default, no ad model, self-hosting capability, the refusal to build backdoors, the explicit public commitment to challenge legal demands that require us to break encryption, is downstream of a set of values that the EFF has been defending in courts and legislatures for 35 years and that Citizen Lab researchers have been documenting with technical rigor for over two decades.

When we say "we will challenge orders that require us to break encryption," that's not just a blog post commitment. That kind of fight costs money, takes expertise, and requires allies who've done it before. The EFF has done it before. Many times.

When we describe the threat of state-sponsored surveillance tools to our users, we're describing what the Citizen Lab has already found in the wild and documented with evidence. They aren't writing hypotheticals; they're publishing the IP addresses, malware samples, network infrastructure, and victim lists.

And when we run infrastructure to make all of this possible, we'd rather do it in a way that doesn't quietly punt the environmental cost to future generations.

## The Pledge

Effective today, Concord Voice commits to:

**Donate a percentage of quarterly revenue to the EFF and the Citizen Lab Fund on a rotating quarterly basis.** A minimum of 1% of quarterly gross revenue is allocated to one organization each quarter, alternating between the Electronic Frontier Foundation and the Citizen Lab Fund. Q1 and Q3 go to one; Q2 and Q4 go to the other. Over the course of a year, both receive equal support. This isn't a one-time thing. It's a line item in our budget, not a discretionary contribution that disappears when margins get tight.

**Contribute to carbon removal through Stripe Climate**, from revenue remaining after monthly operational costs are covered. The contribution runs automatically and is not contingent on quarterly targets or executive approval.

**Grow the contributions as we grow.** As the platform scales, the contributions scale. We're not going to build a profitable company on these principles and then quietly reduce our commitment to the organizations and causes that back them up.

**Publish it quarterly.** Alongside each quarterly transparency report, we'll publish which organization received the contribution that quarter and the exact amount. Not a vague "we support these organizations." Actual numbers, four times a year. You can hold us to it.

**List all three as partners publicly.** Not as a marketing exercise, as an acknowledgment that this work is collaborative. The EFF does the legal and policy work. The Citizen Lab does the technical research and accountability work. Stripe Climate funds the carbon removal. We build the infrastructure. All of it matters.

## Why These Specifically

There are many organizations doing good work in the privacy and climate spaces. We're not saying these are the only ones worth supporting; in fact, we'd encourage you to look at the ACLU's digital rights work, Access Now, Privacy International, and others, depending on your jurisdiction and priorities.

We chose the EFF because they're the ones in the room when the laws are being written, in the courts when the cases are being litigated, and on the ground when the education needs to happen. They're not a think tank that publishes reports. They're an organization that files briefs, wins cases, and ships code. They've also criticized Apple, Google, Meta, and others when those companies compromised on privacy, even when those companies were nominally "on the same side." The privacy space is rife with organizations that won't criticize the companies that fund them. The EFF isn't one of them.

We chose the Citizen Lab because they're the reason "just trust us" no longer works as a surveillance policy argument. Their research is peer-reviewed, forensically grounded, and politically independent. They've embarrassed governments that bought surveillance tools with taxpayer money and used them on their own citizens. They've given journalists and activists the evidence they need to understand who was targeting them and how. That kind of independent technical accountability is irreplaceable, and it doesn't fund itself.

We chose Stripe Climate because it's a straightforward, verifiable way to direct money toward permanent carbon removal rather than creative accounting and it integrates directly with our payment processing. The projects they fund are selected by an independent panel of scientific experts, the methodology is public, and contributions are traceable. We're not trying to claim a carbon-neutral status we haven't earned, but we are trying to make a consistent, honest contribution toward a problem our infrastructure helps create.

## If You Want to Do the Same

You don't have to be a company to support this work. The EFF accepts individual donations and memberships at [eff.org](http://eff.org). The Citizen Lab Fund accepts donations through the University of Toronto Foundation at [citizenlab.ca](http://citizenlab.ca). Worth noting for U.S. donors: because the Citizen Lab is a Canadian charity, your donation is only tax-deductible on a U.S. return if you have Canadian-source income, Canadian wages, dividends, capital gains, and similar. If that doesn't apply to you, the contribution isn't deductible, but it's still real money going to real research. See the disclosure at the bottom for the full details. Stripe Climate's portfolio and contribution details are public at [stripe.com/climate/contribute](http://stripe.com/climate/contribute).

We're not going to tell you what to do with your money. But if you've ever been glad that someone was in a courtroom fighting for your right to have a private conversation, or publishing forensic proof that your government's surveillance tools were being used against people like you, or pulling carbon out of the air, one of those very likely could’ve been them.
